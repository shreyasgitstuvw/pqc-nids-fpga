# Interface Contract — Design Rationale
## Why Each Decision Was Made, and What to Look At Before Confirming

This is the companion document to `interface_contract.md`. For every choice in that draft, this explains *why* it was proposed that way, and for every `[MEMBER A: CONFIRM]` item, it lays out exactly what evidence or conversation should inform the final call — so confirming isn't a guess, it's a decision made on the same reasoning basis as everything else in the contract.

---

## 1. Physical link and framing

### Decision: Pmod USB-UART, not the onboard USB-UART
**Reasoning:** This isn't actually a choice — it's a hardware constraint stated directly in the synopsis (§7.1): the ZedBoard's onboard USB-UART is wired to the processor's dedicated pins and physically unreachable from the programmable logic where this whole design lives. Stated here anyway because it needs to be in the contract explicitly — a teammate skimming only the contract (not the synopsis) shouldn't be able to wire against the wrong UART.

### Decision: 3 Mbaud, 8N1
**Reasoning:** 3 Mbaud matches the synopsis's own timing table (§6.3) — the "link time" figures for encapsulation/decapsulation are computed against this exact baud rate, so changing it invalidates numbers already committed to in the project's own documentation. 8 data bits / no parity / 1 stop bit is the standard, simplest UART framing and there's no stated reason in the synopsis to deviate.
**What to look at before confirming:** Nothing to reconsider here unless your actual FTDI FT232RQ hardware has a specific reason it can't hold 3 Mbaud reliably (some USB-UART bridges have baud-rate accuracy issues at high rates) — if you see framing errors in early bring-up that look like baud-rate drift rather than logic bugs, that's the one reason to revisit this number, and if you do, the synopsis's §6.3 timing table needs updating to match.

### Decision: length-prefixed framing over delimiter-based framing
**Reasoning:** A delimiter scheme (special byte sequence marks packet boundaries) needs an escaping mechanism for when real data happens to contain that same sequence — that's extra logic and an extra failure mode for no real benefit here, since we're not dealing with an adversarial *framing* layer (the adversary in this project tampers with packet *content*, not the framing convention itself, and CRC/crypto layers already exist to catch content tampering). Length-prefix is simpler to implement as a counter-driven state machine and matches how the synopsis already describes packet sizes as roughly fixed/predictable (800-byte encapsulation key, 768-byte ciphertext) rather than needing arbitrary-length delimiter scanning.
**What to look at before confirming the length-field width:** Check the actual maximum packet size across every packet type your pipeline needs to carry — handshake packets (800-byte key, 768-byte ciphertext per synopsis §6.1) plus whatever your largest data payload will be in the test network. A 2-byte length field caps you at 65,535 bytes, which covers the stated handshake sizes with enormous headroom; only widen it if you've deliberately planned for payloads anywhere near that ceiling, which seems unlikely for this project's scope.
**What to look at before confirming truncation/timeout behavior:** This is really a question about what "acceptable latency for a bad frame to clear the pipeline" means for your test network — a very short timeout risks false-triggering on a legitimately slow sender; a long timeout means a truncated frame sits in the deframer for a while before being cleared. Since your test network uses laptops over a point-to-point UART link (not a lossy multi-hop network), truncation should be rare — pick a timeout generous enough to not false-trigger under normal PySerial send timing, and treat frequent `FRAME_TIMEOUT` events during testing as a signal your producer-side script is misbehaving, not that the timeout is miscalibrated.

## 2. Byte order — big-endian throughout

**Reasoning:** Ethernet, IPv4, and TCP/UDP headers are *already* transmitted in big-endian ("network byte order") on real networks — this isn't a project decision, it's how these protocols have worked for decades, and deviating would mean your parser doesn't actually implement the standards it claims to. The decision that *is* yours is extending the same convention to the fields you're defining fresh (`session_id`, `payload_len`, `packet_type`) — done here for one reason: **consistency eliminates an entire class of bug.** If some fields are big-endian (forced, by protocol) and others are little-endian (because that's a common FPGA/software default), every module that touches multiple field types needs to remember which convention applies to which field — a silent, easy-to-miss source of exactly the kind of bug the theory guide's endianness section warned about.
**What to look at before confirming:** There's genuinely nothing to reconsider on the protocol-mandated fields — that's not negotiable. For the fresh fields, the only reason to break from big-endian-everywhere would be if some other tool in your pipeline (a Python library, a debugging utility) assumes little-endian and fighting it constantly outweighs the consistency benefit — unlikely, but worth a five-minute check against whatever you're using to inspect raw bytes during debugging (Wireshark, a hex dump utility) to confirm it isn't fighting your convention.

## 3. The internal packet bus struct

### Decision: exact bit widths mirror the real protocol field widths
**Reasoning:** These aren't chosen — Ethernet MAC addresses are 48 bits, IPv4 addresses are 32 bits, ports are 16 bits, etc., by protocol specification. Choosing anything else would mean the parser isn't actually decoding real Ethernet/IPv4/TCP/UDP traffic, which breaks the "synthetic network of two communicating devices" test setup the moment either device sends genuinely standard-conformant packets.
**What to look at before confirming:** Cross-check each field width against whatever you use to *generate* test packets (likely Python's `scapy` or manual byte construction in `send.py`/`attacker.py`) — the widths in the draft assume standard header layouts; if your test-packet generator does anything non-standard (e.g., omitting IP options entirely, which the draft already assumes), confirm the generator and the parser's assumptions genuinely match before you spend time debugging what looks like a parser bug but is actually a test-data mismatch.

### Decision: IPv4 options and non-IPv4 EtherTypes rejected as malformed (proposed)
**Reasoning:** Supporting IPv4 options means variable-length header parsing — the IHL field can indicate a header longer than the standard 20 bytes, and handling that correctly means your parser's state machine needs to branch on header length rather than assuming a fixed offset for where TCP/UDP begins. That's meaningfully more parser complexity for a feature the project's synthetic test network almost certainly doesn't need — you control both endpoints, so you control whether they ever send options. Similarly, ARP/IPv6 support means an entirely separate parsing path your detection and crypto lanes were never designed to consume. Rejecting both as "malformed, drop and count" is the simplest correct behavior given the actual scope.
**What to look at before confirming:** Check whether your test network's send/recv/attacker scripts, or the CIC-IDS2017 traffic Member B is replaying, ever legitimately produce IPv4-options or non-IPv4-EtherType packets. If the CIC-IDS2017 subset includes any such packets as part of *normal* (non-attack) traffic, rejecting them as malformed would create false positives in Member B's detection-accuracy numbers — worth a quick check of the dataset subset before locking this in. If it's clean, the proposed rejection stands with no downside.

### Decision: payload bytes continue streaming on the same `data`/`valid` signals after header fields are latched, rather than a separate byte-stream interface
**Reasoning:** A second parallel interface for payload bytes would mean every downstream module needs two sets of timing assumptions (when are headers valid vs. when is payload valid) instead of one continuous stream. Piggybacking payload on the same bus that carried the header bytes keeps the timing model uniform: headers are valid and held stable from the moment they're parsed through `eof`, and payload bytes continue arriving on `data`/`valid` in the meantime — one mental model for every consumer.
**What to look at before confirming:** This is the field most likely to need Member B and Member C's direct input rather than something you can decide by inspection alone, since they're the ones actually consuming raw payload bytes (Member B for signature matching, Member C for handshake key/ciphertext extraction). Ask them directly whether a single combined bus works for their module's internal buffering strategy, or whether they'd genuinely rather have payload delivered separately — this is worth a real conversation, not just a design review on your end, because the cost of getting it wrong is *their* module needing rework, not yours.

## 4. Packet classification (`packet_type`)

**Reasoning for why this field needs to exist at all:** Nothing in the standard Ethernet/IP/TCP/UDP header set distinguishes "this is a key-exchange handshake" from "this is regular encrypted application data" — that distinction is entirely internal to this project's protocol design, so it has to be invented, not parsed from an existing spec.
**Reasoning for proposing two mechanisms (port-based vs. explicit tag) rather than picking one for you:** This is a genuine design tradeoff, not something with an obviously correct answer:
- Port-based is simpler to parse (you already extract `l4_dst_port` for other reasons) and mirrors how real protocols often reserve well-known ports for control traffic.
- An explicit tag byte is more robust if you ever want handshake and data traffic to share a port, and doesn't depend on the sender picking a "correct" port number — but it costs an extra byte of overhead per packet and a small amount of extra parsing logic to extract it from the payload.

**What to look at before confirming:** This is squarely a three-way decision with Member C and Member D, not something you should finalize unilaterally, because they're the ones whose modules trigger off this field. Concretely: ask Member C whether their `mlkem_top.v` state machine has any preference (state machines are sometimes simpler to trigger off a clean field-compare than parsing a payload byte), and ask Member D whether ChaCha/session_mgr has any assumption baked in already. Whichever mechanism you land on, the actual port number or tag byte value needs to be written into the contract as a concrete constant (not just "port-based" as a category) before this section can be considered frozen — an unspecified magic number is exactly the kind of ambiguity debt this whole document exists to eliminate.

## 5. Session register file

### Decision: session table entry structure (`session_id`, `shared_secret`, `nonce_counter`, `state`)
**Reasoning:** This mirrors the minimum information genuinely needed for the protocol to function: `session_id` to index sessions (supporting more than one at a time, however many the test network needs), `shared_secret` because that's literally the output of Member C's KEM and the input Member D's ChaCha needs, `nonce_counter` because ChaCha20 requires a nonce that must never repeat under the same key (reusing a nonce catastrophically breaks stream-cipher security — this isn't a nice-to-have field, it's a correctness requirement), and `state` because the pipeline's behavior genuinely differs across IDLE/HANDSHAKING/ESTABLISHED/REJECTED and something needs to track which one currently applies.
**What to look at before confirming session table size:** Look directly at the synopsis's test network description (§6.4) — two communicating devices plus one attacker joining the same links. That's fundamentally a single active session at a time in the reference test setup (the attacker doesn't establish its own legitimate session, it injects into or attacks the existing one). A session table with room for a small handful of entries (say, 4) is very likely more than sufficient and avoids over-building — but check whether your specific test plan ever wants multiple simultaneous legitimate sessions (e.g., testing session isolation as a feature) before finalizing at exactly 1.

### Decision (not really yours, but affects your timing): implicit-rejection indistinguishability is called out as a cross-cutting note rather than a rule for your module
**Reasoning:** This requirement genuinely lives in Member C and Member D's modules (the FO transform and session_mgr's state transitions) — your parser and egress logic aren't the ones deciding whether a rejected handshake "looks different" from a successful one. It's flagged in the contract anyway because your egress timing (how fast a response goes back out over UART once a handshake concludes) is a signal an outside observer could theoretically read, so it's worth a shared awareness across the team even though the actual fix isn't in your code.
**What to look at before confirming:** Nothing to decide here — this is informational for you, not an open item requiring your sign-off. The one thing worth doing once integration testing starts is a quick empirical check: does your egress path introduce any *variable* delay based on which internal path (success vs. rejection) triggered it? If your egress logic is uniform regardless of what upstream state produced the outgoing bytes, you're fine by construction and don't need to think about this further.

## 6. Verdict interface and reason codes

### Decision: `{fail, reason_code}` as a flat 1-bit + 4-bit struct, not a richer structure
**Reasoning:** Simplicity. Every lane that can fail a packet needs to communicate exactly two things — did it fail, and why — and a flat struct is the easiest possible thing for `drop_engine.v` to OR together and log. A richer structure (e.g., a severity field, a confidence score) was considered and rejected here because nothing in the synopsis's objectives asks for graded severity — Objective 7 wants failure counts by category, not a scored risk level, so building for that would be speculative complexity.
**What to look at before confirming:** No real open question here unless a specific downstream need emerges (e.g., if the final report specifically wants to distinguish "high confidence" vs. "low confidence" CMS-based flood detection) — if that comes up, it's a scope addition worth a deliberate team conversation, not something to bolt onto this struct informally.

### Decision: 4-bit reason code (16 possible values, 9 currently assigned)
**Reasoning:** 4 bits comfortably covers the current 9 defined codes with headroom for a few more without needing to widen the field later (widening a shared enum's width after modules are built against it is exactly the kind of late-breaking contract change this document is trying to prevent). Going wider (e.g., 8 bits) was considered and rejected as premature — there's no concrete need for 256 reason codes in a project with this scope, and an oversized field just wastes bits on every verdict signal across the whole design, repeated on every single packet.
**What to look at before confirming the specific numeric assignments:** This genuinely needs input from Member B specifically — their theory guide treats `FLOOD` and `SCAN` as related but statistically distinguishable detection categories (different traffic shapes: repeated hits on one target vs. spread across many targets), and the draft here gives them separate codes on the assumption that distinction is worth preserving in the final telemetry. Confirm with Member B whether their Count-Min Sketch design actually produces this distinction cleanly, or whether it's more natural for their module to report a single `VOLUMETRIC` code and let a separate mechanism (if any) distinguish flood from scan. Don't guess on their behalf — this is their detection-accuracy reporting on the line, and the theory guide's language council was written to help you understand the distinction exists, not to dictate that hardware must expose it as two separate codes.

### Decision: verdict latency table left mostly unfilled, explicitly marked as the highest-priority open item
**Reasoning:** This is the one part of the whole contract that's genuinely impossible for you (or me) to fill in correctly in advance, because the actual cycle latency of `count_min_sketch.v`, `poly1305.v`, and `fo_transform.v` depends on implementation choices only Members B, C, and D can make — how many pipeline stages their modules use, whether their computation is fully combinational or registered, etc. Filling these in with guessed numbers would be worse than leaving them explicitly blank, because a wrong number that *looks* authoritative is more dangerous than an acknowledged unknown — someone might build `drop_engine.v` against a guessed latency and only discover the mismatch at Phase VI integration, which is precisely the expensive-to-debug scenario this whole document exists to prevent.
**What to look at before confirming:** This isn't something you look at — it's something you actively collect. Go to each of Members B, C, and D once their respective modules have a first working simulation and ask directly: "how many cycles after your module receives its last input does your verdict signal become valid?" Once you have all three numbers, the follow-up decision (padding all lanes to match the slowest, vs. having `drop_engine.v` track per-lane outstanding status explicitly) is a tradeoff between simplicity (padding) and flexibility (explicit tracking) — lean toward padding unless the slowest lane's latency is dramatically larger than the others, in which case padding wastes too much pipeline time and the more complex tracking approach earns its cost.

### Decision: simultaneous-failure priority — log both codes rather than picking a winner (proposed)
**Reasoning:** Picking a single "winning" reason code when two lanes fail the same packet in the same cycle means you're deciding to *lose information* — the final report's detection-accuracy numbers would then undercount one category whenever it co-occurs with another. Logging both as separate counter increments is strictly more informative and, since `drop_engine.v` already needs to OR the fail bits together to decide whether to drop the packet at all, incrementing two counters instead of one isn't a large additional cost.
**What to look at before confirming:** Ask Member D directly whether logging both complicates their `drop_engine.v` implementation more than the informational benefit is worth — they're the one actually writing the RTL that has to handle this case, and if a same-cycle dual-failure turns out to be rare enough in practice that the extra logic isn't worth it, a documented priority order (e.g., "cryptographic failures always take priority over detection-lane failures in the log, since they represent a more severe class of event") is a completely reasonable fallback. Either choice is defensible — what matters is that whichever one you pick is written down here explicitly, so nobody has to guess or re-derive it during integration.

## Summary: the pattern behind every "why" in this document

Almost every decision above reduces to one of three justifications, worth having as a general lens for evaluating anything not covered here that comes up later:
1. **It's not actually a choice — it's a protocol or hardware constraint** (byte order on standard headers, the Pmod UART requirement, standard field widths). These aren't yours to relitigate; they're facts about the world your parser has to conform to.
2. **It's the simplest option that fully covers the project's actual stated scope** (length-prefixed framing, rejecting IPv4 options, a flat verdict struct) — chosen not because it's the most general solution, but because building for a broader scope than the synopsis actually needs is speculative complexity with no clear payoff.
3. **It genuinely can't be decided in isolation and needs input from whoever's module is most affected** (packet_type mechanism, reason-code granularity, verdict latencies) — these are flagged, not guessed, because a wrong guess here is expensive precisely at the moment (Phase VI integration) when it's hardest to trace back to its source.
